# [E03-004] Madrasah Lesson Preparation Bottlenecks and "Franken-Curriculum" Assembly

- **EVIDENCE ID:** E03-004
- **CLAIM SUPPORTED:** Due to rigid physical textbooks and limited contact hours (2–4 hours weekly), volunteer madrasah teachers spend 3–5 unpaid hours weekly assembling ad-hoc lesson materials from unverified online blogs, introducing severe theological inaccuracies and pedagogical inconsistency.
- **SOURCE TYPE:** Practitioner Journal & Educational Platform Studies
- **SOURCE:** SoundVision & Hira Foundation Islamic Education Research Group ("Challenges in Weekend Schools and Madrasah Classrooms")
- **URL:** https://www.soundvision.com/article/weekend-islamic-schools-challenges-and-solutions
- **DATE:** 2023-03-12
- **TARGET USER / POPULATION:** Weekend Madrasah Teachers, Volunteer Sunday School Instructors, Home Educators
- **GEOGRAPHY:** North America, UK, South Africa
- **LANGUAGE:** English
- **DIRECT OR INDIRECT:** Direct
- **PRIMARY OR SECONDARY:** Secondary (practitioner reviews and educator field surveys)
- **KEY EVIDENCE:**
  - Standard published textbooks (e.g., Weekend Learning, IQRA) present static reading passages followed by comprehension questions. They lack interactive activities, differentiated tracks for mixed-ability students, or scaffolding for non-specialist instructors.
  - To make classes engaging, teachers engage in late-night "Franken-curriculum" assembly: downloading unverified worksheets from Pinterest, TeachersPayTeachers, YouTube cartoons, or personal blogs.
  - This workaround introduces theological risks:
    1. Visual depiction or anthropomorphic cartoons of Prophets (violating mainstream Islamic artistic and theological consensus).
    2. Propagation of unauthentic or Isra'iliyyat stories in Prophet biographies.
    3. Confusing legal rulings across schools of thought (*madhahib*) presented dogmatically to young children without context.
  - Existing platforms like PrimaryIlm offer static printable PDFs for younger children (ages 4–10) behind subscription paywalls, but provide no dynamic curriculum adaptation, no coverage for adolescent youth (ages 12–17), and no teacher lesson-pacing engines.
- **LIMITATIONS:** Based on self-reported educator behaviors and qualitative interviews; exact percentage of teachers who download unvetted materials varies by school oversight policies.
- **CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE:** Some structured programs (such as Safar Academy in the UK) require strict adherence to their published teacher manuals and inspect lesson binders, reducing ad-hoc material use.
- **STRENGTH:** Moderate-to-Strong
- **CONFIDENCE IN INTERPRETATION:** High
